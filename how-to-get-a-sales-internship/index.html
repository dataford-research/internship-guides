Sales internships

How to get a sales internship

The titles to search, the types of sales roles, the skills to build, the interview, and where to find sales internships.

By Amney Mounir. Published November 16, 2026.

Sales internships are some of the easiest internships to land and some of the most useful to have. Almost every company needs people who can talk to customers, and many of them hire interns every summer to help with outreach, research, and small deals. You do not need a business major or a long resume. You need to show you can talk to people, handle a "no," and keep going. Here is how I would go after a sales internship as a student today.

Know the titles

Sales roles go by a lot of names. Search for sales intern, sales development intern, business development intern, account management intern, inside sales intern, customer success intern, partnerships intern, and sales operations intern. At software companies, the entry role is usually called a sales development representative (SDR) or business development representative (BDR), and many of them run intern programs that feed straight into that job.

Understand the main types of sales internships

The day-to-day work depends a lot on what the company sells:

  • Software and tech sales: researching companies, writing outreach emails, booking meetings for account executives, and learning the CRM.
  • Consumer goods and retail: working with stores and distributors, checking shelves and displays, and tracking sales by region.
  • Medical and industrial sales: supporting reps who sell equipment or supplies to hospitals, labs, and factories. Great for science and engineering majors.
  • Financial services and insurance: helping advisors and agents find and serve clients.
  • Field and territory sales: driving a route, visiting customers, and running a small territory, often with a clear commission or bonus plan.

Pick one or two that fit your interests, but apply across a few. A summer in any of them teaches the same core skills.

Build the skills sales teams look for

Hiring managers for sales interns care less about your GPA and more about how you communicate. You can build that now:

  • Get comfortable talking to strangers: a campus job, a retail or restaurant job, fundraising, or tabling for a club all count
  • Practice writing short, clear emails that ask for one thing
  • Learn the basics of a CRM like HubSpot or Salesforce. Both have free training
  • Learn how to research a company quickly: what it sells, who its customers are, and what changed recently
  • Get used to hearing "no" and following up politely

If you have ever sold anything, from club tickets to items on a resale site, that is real sales experience. Put it on your resume.

Write a resume that shows drive

Keep it to one page. Sales managers look for numbers and effort. Lead each bullet with a result, like "raised about $3,000 for a club event by calling 60+ local businesses" or "brought in 25 new members through outreach at orientation." Show any job where you worked with customers, any goal you hit, and any competitive activity, like sports, debate, or case competitions.

Network like a salesperson

The best way to prove you can sell is to sell yourself well. Find sales reps and managers at companies you like on LinkedIn, send a short note asking for 15 minutes, and come with good questions about their work. Ask how they hit their numbers, what a normal week looks like, and what they look for in interns. Many sales leaders will refer a student who reaches out with a clear, polite ask, because that is exactly the behavior they want on their team.

Prepare for the interview

Sales internship interviews are usually short and practical:

  1. Behavioral questions. Have stories ready about a time you persuaded someone, missed a goal and recovered, and worked hard on something nobody asked you to do.
  2. Company questions. Know what the company sells, who buys it, and one or two competitors.
  3. A role-play. A classic one is "sell me this pen." Ask questions about the buyer before you pitch, connect the product to what they need, and ask for the sale at the end.

Bring energy, keep your answers short, and follow up with a thank-you email the same day. In sales, the follow-up is part of the test.

Apply beyond the famous names

The big software and consumer brands get the most applications, but there are thousands of sales internships at mid-size software companies, distributors, manufacturers, banks, insurance firms, and local businesses. Many of them are only posted on the company's own careers page.

To find them, I Got An Internship is a free internship search tool that pulls openings straight from about 2,200 employer career pages across the US and Canada. Search for sales, business development, or customer success internships and you will see roles that never make it to the big job boards.

Timing

Large companies with formal sales intern programs often recruit in the fall for the next summer. Smaller companies hire from January through April, and some take sales interns during the school year too. Apply to the big programs early and keep checking smaller companies every week through spring.

A simple plan

  • Pick one or two types of sales that interest you
  • Get one customer-facing job or project on your resume
  • Learn the basics of one CRM
  • Reach out to at least five sales reps or managers
  • Practice "sell me this pen" and two or three behavioral stories
  • Apply to 40 or more roles across different industries and titles
