How to get a software engineering internship

When to apply, what projects to build, how to prep for coding interviews, and where to find companies beyond big tech.

By Amney Mounir. Published November 16, 2026.

Software engineering internships are some of the most competitive roles students apply for. Big tech companies get thousands of applications for each opening, and most of them open early. The good news is that there are a lot more companies hiring software interns than the ten names everyone applies to. Here is how I would approach it.

Start earlier than you think

Many large tech companies open summer internship applications in July, August, and September of the year before. By October and November, a lot of the big programs have already started interviews. Smaller companies and startups hire later, often from January through April, so if you missed the early wave you still have plenty of options.

A simple timeline:

  • Summer: polish your resume and one or two projects
  • August to October: apply to large companies and banks with tech roles
  • November to February: keep applying, practice interviews
  • February to April: focus on startups, mid-size companies, and local employers

Build one or two real projects

If you have no past internship, projects are what fill your resume. One finished project you can explain in detail is worth more than five half-built ones.

Good project ideas are small apps that solve a real problem for you or people you know: a tool for your club, a script that saves you time, a simple web app with a database and login. Put the code on GitHub with a short README that explains what it does, how to run it, and what you learned.

Keep your resume to one page

Recruiters skim a software resume in seconds. Put these in order:

  • Education, with your expected graduation date
  • Skills, listing only languages and tools you can actually use
  • Projects, each with one or two lines on what you built and the result
  • Any work experience, even if it is not technical

Use numbers where you can, like "cut page load time by half" or "used by about 200 students". Save it as a PDF and name the file with your name.

Practice coding interviews

Most software internship interviews include one or two coding problems. Usually these are data structures and algorithms questions: arrays, strings, hash maps, trees, and graphs. You do not need to solve hundreds of problems. Working through 50 to 100 common ones, and being able to explain your thinking out loud, is enough for most intern interviews.

Practice talking while you code. Interviewers care about how you think through a problem, not only whether you get the final answer.

Apply to more than big tech

Everyone applies to the same few famous companies. Thousands of other employers also hire software interns: banks, insurance companies, retailers, hospitals, government contractors, game studios, and startups. These roles often get far fewer applicants and still give you real engineering experience that helps you land a bigger name next year.

To find them, I Got An Internship is a free internship search tool that pulls openings straight from about 2,200 employer career pages across the US and Canada. Search for software, engineering, or developer internships in your city and you will see companies you may never have thought to check.

Send enough applications

For software internships, it is normal to send 50 to 100 applications to get a handful of interviews. Keep a simple tracker with the company, the date you applied, and the status. Follow up when you can find a real person on the team, and do not take silence personally. It is a numbers game for almost everyone.

Use your network

Referrals help a lot in tech. Ask friends who interned last summer, upperclassmen in your major, and alumni on LinkedIn whether their company is hiring interns. A short, specific message works better than a long one. Career fairs and hackathons are also good places to meet engineers and recruiters in person.

Quick checklist

  • Start applying in late summer and keep going through spring
  • Finish one or two projects and put them on GitHub
  • Keep your resume to one page with clear results
  • Practice 50 to 100 common coding problems out loud
  • Apply well beyond the famous tech companies
  • Send 50 to 100 applications and track them
  • Ask for referrals from people you know
