How to write an internship resume with no experience

What to put first, how projects and part-time jobs count, and a short checklist before you hit submit.

By Amney Mounir. Published November 16, 2026.

Most students applying for their first internship have the same worry: there is nothing to put on the resume. That is normal. Employers hiring interns know you are early in your career. What they want to see is proof that you can learn, finish things, and work with other people. You probably have more of that than you think.

Keep it to one page

One page, simple layout, no photo, no graphics. Use a standard font and clear section headings. Many employers run applications through an applicant tracking system first, and plain formatting reads cleanly in those systems.

A simple order that works for most students:

  1. Name and contact details (email, phone, city, LinkedIn or portfolio link)
  2. Education
  3. Projects
  4. Experience (any kind)
  5. Skills
  6. Activities and leadership

Lead with education

With no internships yet, education is your strongest section, so put it first. Include your school, degree, major, and expected graduation month and year. Add your GPA if it is 3.0 or higher. List two to four relevant courses if they line up with the role, for example statistics and SQL for a data internship.

Projects count as experience

This is the section most students skip and the one that helps the most. A class project, a hackathon, a personal app, a research paper, a club budget you rebuilt in a spreadsheet: all of these show real work.

For each project, write two or three bullets that answer:

  • What did you build or do?
  • What tools or skills did you use?
  • What was the result? Use a number when you honestly have one, like users, hours saved, or a grade.

Example: "Built a Python script that cleaned and merged 3 years of club attendance data, cutting the monthly report from 4 hours to 20 minutes."

Any job is worth listing

Retail, food service, tutoring, babysitting, campus jobs, and volunteering all belong on the resume. Focus the bullets on skills that carry over: customer communication, handling money, training new staff, showing up reliably, solving problems under pressure.

Skills: be specific and honest

List tools and languages you can actually use, such as Excel, Google Sheets, Python, SQL, Figma, or a second language. Skip vague soft skills like "hard worker" and show those through your bullets instead. Only list what you would be comfortable being asked about in an interview.

Match each resume to the posting

Read the internship posting and pick out the top three skills it asks for. Make sure those show up on your resume, in the employer's own words where they honestly describe what you did. You do not need a brand new resume for every application, but a few minutes of tailoring goes a long way.

Then apply widely

A good resume only helps if it is in front of enough employers. Most students need somewhere around 30 to 80 applications in a season, depending on year and field.

A quick checklist before you submit

  • One page, saved as a PDF with your name in the file name
  • Education at the top, with graduation date
  • At least two projects with results
  • Every job or volunteer role listed with transferable skills
  • Skills you can actually talk about
  • No typos (read it out loud once)

Always confirm the requirements, dates, and pay on the employer's own posting before you apply.
