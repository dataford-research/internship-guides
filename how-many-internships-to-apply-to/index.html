How many internships should you apply to

Rough ranges by year and field, a simple way to work backwards from interviews, and how to spread applications across the season.

By Amney Mounir. Published November 16, 2026.

This is one of the questions I get most from students. The honest answer depends on your year, your field, and how strong your materials are. But there are some useful ranges, and a simple way to figure out your own number.

A rough starting range

For most undergraduates looking for a summer internship, I would plan on somewhere between 30 and 80 applications in a season. Here is how that range usually breaks down:

  • First and second year students: 30 to 50 applications. Fewer employers hire early, so cast a wide net and include smaller companies, labs, nonprofits, and local employers.
  • Third year students in competitive fields (finance, consulting, big tech): 50 to 100+ applications. These programs get thousands of applicants per seat and recruit early.
  • Students with a referral or a return offer path: fewer applications, more networking. A warm introduction is worth a lot of cold applications.

These are not targets to hit for the sake of it. Twenty careful applications to roles you actually fit will beat a hundred rushed ones.

Work backwards from interviews

The easier way to pick your number is to work backwards. Most students I talk to see something like a 5 to 10 percent response rate on cold online applications. If you want three or four first-round interviews, that points to roughly 40 to 60 applications.

Track your own numbers. After your first 15 or 20 applications, check how many turned into a reply. If you are getting zero responses, the fix is usually your resume or the roles you are targeting, not sending more of the same.

Quality checks before you hit submit

Before each application, I would check three things:

  1. Do you meet the basic requirements? Year in school, major, graduation date, and work authorization. If the posting says it needs a junior and you are a first year, skip it.
  2. Does your resume show the top two or three skills in the posting? Use the employer's own words where they honestly describe what you did.
  3. Is the posting still open? Old listings get recycled on job boards long after the employer has filled the role. Always confirm on the employer's own careers page.

Spread applications across the season

Do not send all 50 applications in one weekend. Hiring windows differ by industry. Finance and consulting can open more than a year ahead, while many technology, engineering, and nonprofit roles open in the fall and keep posting through spring.

A steady pace works better: five to ten applications a week from September through March, plus follow-ups on anything older than two weeks.

Where to find enough good roles

Hitting 40 or more quality applications means you need a steady supply of real, open roles. Your school's career platform, employer career pages, and career fairs are a good base.

I built I Got An Internship to make this part faster. It is a free internship search platform that pulls internships and co-ops straight from about 2,200 employer career pages across the US and Canada, about 5,000 roles at a time. You can filter by role, term, city, and visa sponsorship, and listings come down when the employer removes the posting, so you are not wasting applications on roles that already closed.

Keep a tracker

Once you pass 20 applications, you will not remember where each one stands. Keep one spreadsheet with company, role, link, date applied, status, and a follow-up date. It also shows you which kinds of roles and sources actually get you interviews, so you can adjust your number as you go.

Always confirm dates, eligibility, and pay on the employer's own posting before you apply.
