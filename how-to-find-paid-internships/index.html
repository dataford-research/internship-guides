How to find paid internships

Which fields usually pay, how to spot unpaid roles early, where paid programs get posted first, and when to apply.

By Amney Mounir. Published November 16, 2026.

A lot of students assume they have to choose between an internship that looks good on a resume and a job that pays rent. In most fields you do not. Plenty of employers pay their interns, and many pay well. The trick is knowing where those roles are posted and how to spot the unpaid ones early so you do not waste time on them. Here is how I would go about it.

Know which fields usually pay

Pay varies a lot by industry. Software engineering, data, finance, consulting, engineering, and most large corporate internship programs are almost always paid, often hourly at a rate well above minimum wage. Government, nonprofit, media, fashion, museums, and the arts are where you are more likely to see unpaid or stipend-only roles.

That does not mean you should avoid those fields. It means you should look harder for the paid programs inside them, and plan ahead, because the paid ones fill fast.

Check the rules for unpaid internships

In the US, for-profit companies can only offer unpaid internships if the intern is the main one benefiting from the arrangement, which usually means it is tied to school credit and real training, not regular work. If a company wants you doing the same work as paid staff for free, that is a red flag. In Canada the rules depend on the province, and many unpaid internships are only allowed when they are part of a school program.

You do not need to be a lawyer about it. Just know that "unpaid, 40 hours a week, answering phones" at a for-profit company is not normal, and you can walk away.

Search company career pages directly

The best paid internships are usually posted on the employer's own careers page first, sometimes weeks before they show up anywhere else. Large companies run structured programs with set pay, housing help, and a clear timeline, and they post them on their own sites.

Make a list of 20 to 30 companies you would actually like to work for and check their careers pages every week or two during recruiting season. Look for words like "intern," "co-op," "summer analyst," or "new grad" in the job titles.

This is the reason I built I Got An Internship. It is a free internship search tool that pulls openings straight from about 2,200 employer career pages across the US and Canada, about 5,000 roles at a time, with filters for role, term, city, and visa sponsorship. Since the listings come from real employers, most of them are paid roles at companies with actual intern programs.

Read the posting for pay details

More US states and cities now require employers to list a pay range in job postings, so you will often see an hourly rate right in the description. If the posting says "stipend," "school credit," "volunteer," or "unpaid," you know where it stands. If it says nothing at all, it is fine to ask the recruiter once you are in the process:

"Could you share the hourly rate or pay range for this internship?"

That is a normal question. Recruiters get it all the time.

Look at co-ops and university programs

Co-op programs, where you work full time for a semester as part of your degree, are almost always paid and often run longer than a summer internship, so you earn more and learn more. Your school's career center and co-op office will know which employers hire through them.

Also check your school's job board. Employers that post there are usually trying to recruit from your campus specifically, so there is less competition than on big national job boards.

Apply early

Paid internships at large companies tend to open in late summer and fall for the following summer, and many fill on a rolling basis. If you wait until spring, a lot of the best paid programs will already be closed. Start your search in August or September, keep a simple tracker, and apply to the roles you want within the first couple of weeks they are open.

If you take an unpaid role anyway

Sometimes an unpaid or low-paid role is the right call, for example a short internship in a field where paid roles are rare. If you do take one, ask about school credit, a stipend, travel or lunch reimbursement, and flexible hours so you can keep a paying part-time job. Many schools also offer grants for students in unpaid summer internships, so ask your career center before you say no.

Quick checklist

  • Know which fields usually pay and which ones you will need to dig into
  • Skip for-profit roles that want regular work for free
  • Check 20 to 30 company career pages every week or two
  • Read postings for a pay range, and ask the recruiter if there is none
  • Look at co-ops and your school's own job board
  • Start in August or September, not spring
  • If you go unpaid, ask about credit, stipends, and school grants
