Marketing internships

How to get a marketing internship

Which kind of marketing to aim for, a small portfolio that proves it, the interview, and where to find roles beyond big brands.

By Amney Mounir. Published November 16, 2026.

Marketing internships are some of the most common internships out there. Almost every company needs help with social media, email, content, events, or growth, and many of them hire students to do it. I work in product growth at Meta, which sits close to marketing, so this is what I would tell a student who wants a marketing internship next summer. You do not need a marketing major. You need proof that you can make something, measure it, and explain what happened.

Know which kind of marketing you want

"Marketing intern" can mean very different jobs. Before you apply, pick one or two of these to focus on:

  • Social media: planning posts, writing captions, editing short videos, and tracking engagement.
  • Content: writing blog posts, newsletters, and website copy.
  • Growth or performance marketing: running paid ads, testing landing pages, and reading the numbers.
  • Brand and communications: campaigns, PR, partnerships, and events.
  • Marketing analytics: dashboards, reporting, and figuring out which channels work.

You can still apply broadly, but knowing your focus helps you pick the right projects and write a sharper resume.

Build a small portfolio

Marketing hiring managers want to see your work. A simple portfolio beats a long list of skills. Some ideas that take a few weekends:

  • Run the social media account for a club or a friend's small business for a month and track follower growth and engagement.
  • Start a newsletter or blog on a topic you care about and share how many people read it.
  • Write three sample posts or ads for a brand you like, with a short note on who they are for and why.
  • Take a free Google Analytics or Meta ads course and write up one thing you would test.

Put it all on one simple page, like a free personal site or a shared doc, and link it at the top of your resume. Numbers help a lot, even small ones. "Grew a club Instagram from 300 to 900 followers in one semester" is more convincing than "strong social media skills."

Make your resume about results

Every line on your resume should show what you did and what changed. For example:

  • Wrote and scheduled 40 Instagram posts for a campus club, raising average likes by 60%
  • Planned a fundraising event promoted through email and social, with 150+ attendees
  • Built a Google Sheets report tracking weekly newsletter opens and clicks

Retail, food service, and tutoring jobs count too. Talking to customers and understanding what they want is a big part of marketing.

Prepare for the interview

Marketing intern interviews are usually friendly and practical. Expect questions like:

  1. "Tell me about a brand whose marketing you like and why." Pick one and be specific about a campaign or post.
  2. "How would you grow our social media or email list?" Give two or three concrete ideas and say how you would measure them.
  3. "Tell me about a time you worked on a team or met a deadline." Have two short stories ready.

Look at the company's website, social accounts, and recent campaigns before the interview. Coming in with one small, thoughtful idea for them makes a strong impression.

Look past the big brands

Big consumer brands and agencies get flooded with applications. There are many more marketing internships at startups, local businesses, nonprofits, hospitals, universities, and B2B software companies. Search for different titles too: marketing intern, social media intern, content intern, communications intern, growth intern, and digital marketing intern.

To find them, I Got An Internship is a free internship search tool that pulls openings straight from about 2,200 employer career pages across the US and Canada. Search for marketing internships in your city and you will see companies that never post on the big job boards.

Timing

Large companies and agencies often post summer marketing internships in the fall, from September to November. Startups and smaller companies hire later, often from February to April, and some hire for spring or fall internships too. Apply to the big programs early and keep checking smaller companies every week.

A simple plan

  • Pick one or two areas of marketing to focus on
  • Build a small portfolio with real numbers
  • Write a one-page resume with results on every line
  • Study the company before each interview and bring one idea
  • Apply to 50 or more roles across startups, nonprofits, and local companies
