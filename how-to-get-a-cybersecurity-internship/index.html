Cybersecurity internships

How to get a cybersecurity internship

The titles to search, the basics to learn, how to get hands-on, the interview, and where to find security roles.

By Amney Mounir. Published November 16, 2026.

Cybersecurity internships are easier to land than most students think, but only if you can show hands-on work. Security teams want to know you can actually poke at a system, read logs, and explain what you found. Here is how I would go after a cybersecurity internship as a student today.

Know the titles

Security roles go by a lot of names. Search for cybersecurity intern, information security intern, security analyst intern, SOC intern, security engineering intern, GRC intern, IT risk intern, penetration testing intern, and cloud security intern. GRC (governance, risk, and compliance) and IT audit roles often get fewer applicants and are a solid way in.

Learn the basics that show up in every interview

You do not need to know everything, but you should be comfortable with:

  • Networking basics: TCP/IP, DNS, HTTP, ports, and what a firewall does
  • Linux command line: moving around, reading logs, permissions
  • How common attacks work: phishing, SQL injection, weak passwords, misconfigured cloud storage
  • One scripting language, usually Python or Bash
  • What a SIEM is and how a SOC analyst triages an alert

Get hands-on

Hiring managers in security care a lot about what you have done outside class. A few easy ways to build that:

  • Capture the flag (CTF) competitions, solo or with a campus club
  • Free practice labs like TryHackMe, Hack The Box, or OverTheWire
  • A home lab: a couple of virtual machines, a firewall, and a log collector
  • Writeups of what you did, posted on a blog or GitHub

A short writeup of how you solved a CTF challenge is a great thing to link on your resume.

Consider an entry-level certification

Certifications are not required for most internships, but one can help if you have no security experience yet. CompTIA Security+ is the most common. Some schools and programs offer free or discounted vouchers, so check with your department before paying.

Write a resume that shows your work

Keep it to one page. Put a projects section near the top and say what you did and what came out of it:

  • "Built a home lab with 3 virtual machines and set up log collection to detect brute-force logins"
  • "Placed in the top 10% of 1,000+ teams in a national CTF competition"
  • "Wrote a Python script that flags weak passwords in a test user list"

Prepare for the interview

Most cybersecurity internship interviews include:

  1. Technical questions. Expect things like "what happens when you type a URL into a browser," "what is the difference between symmetric and asymmetric encryption," and "how would you investigate a suspicious login."
  2. A scenario. You may be asked how you would respond to a phishing report or a malware alert. Walk through it step by step and say what you would check first.
  3. Behavioral questions. Have stories ready about a hard problem you solved, a time you made a mistake, and how you keep learning.

It is fine to say "I don't know, but here is how I would find out." Security teams value that.

Apply beyond the big names

Big tech security internships get a lot of applicants. Banks, insurance companies, hospitals, utilities, government contractors, consulting firms, and universities all hire security interns too, and many post those roles only on their own career pages.

To find them, I Got An Internship is a free internship search tool that pulls openings straight from about 2,200 employer career pages across the US and Canada. Search for security or cybersecurity internships and you will see roles that never make it to the big job boards.

Timing

Large companies and government contractors often open security internships in late summer and early fall, and some government roles need extra time for background checks. Smaller companies hire later, often in the spring. If you miss the first wave, keep doing labs and keep applying.

A simple plan

  • Pick the titles you will search for, including GRC and IT audit
  • Learn networking, Linux, and one scripting language
  • Do a few CTFs or practice labs and write up what you did
  • Put your projects near the top of a one-page resume
  • Practice explaining a security scenario out loud
  • Apply to 40 or more roles, including banks, hospitals, and government contractors
