Work authorization

How to find internships that mention visa sponsorship

Filter early, then read the eligibility language on the employer's own posting. A practical search guide for students who need work-authorization support.

By Amney Mounir. Published November 16, 2026.

If you need work authorization support, the internship search has an extra step: you have to read the eligibility language on every posting, not just the role title and city. Some employers say they will consider sponsorship. Many say they cannot. A large number do not say anything at all, which is its own kind of signal that you should verify before you invest a long application.

This is a practical search guide, not legal advice. Your international student office, or an immigration attorney, is the place to go for questions about your own status, including CPT, OPT, or other permission to work in the United States or Canada.

Separate two questions

An employer's public sponsorship policy and your permission to work are different things.

  • Sponsorship is what the employer is willing to do, such as considering CPT or OPT, or filing for a future work visa.
  • Authorization is what you already have, or can obtain through your school, for a specific internship period.

A posting that says "must be authorized to work without sponsorship" is telling you about the employer's policy. It does not change what your school can approve. A posting that is silent on sponsorship does not mean the team can hire you. You still need to confirm both sides.

Read the posting carefully

Work-authorization language is often near the bottom, in a requirements list, or in a legal paragraph under the benefits. Look for phrases such as:

  • "Must be legally authorized to work in the United States without sponsorship"
  • "This role is not eligible for visa sponsorship"
  • "Students on CPT or OPT are welcome to apply"
  • "We will consider candidates who will need sponsorship"

If the language is unclear, a short, polite question to the recruiter or the internship program email can save you time: "I am a student at [school] and would complete this internship under CPT. Does this posting accept that?"

Keep a note of the exact wording in your tracker, along with the date you checked. Policies can differ by team, even inside the same company.

Filter early, then verify

Start with your school's career platform and any international-student job lists your campus maintains. Those employers have often already been asked the sponsorship question.

Then use a search tool that lets you filter for visa sponsorship, and still open the employer's own posting before you apply. I built I Got An Internship as a free internship search platform for students. It pulls internships and co-ops from about 2,200 employer career pages in the US and Canada. Students can filter by role, term, city and visa sponsorship, which is a useful first cut when you would otherwise open hundreds of listings by hand.

A filter is a starting point. The employer's current careers page is the source of truth. If the listing and the career-page language disagree, trust the career page and write down what you found.

Watch for common mismatches

  • Graduation year and return-to-school rules. Some internships are only for students who will go back to campus afterward. That can matter for OPT timing.
  • Location and remote work. A role that looks remote may still require you to be in a specific country for tax or export-control reasons.
  • Security, government, and some research labs. Citizenship or clearance rules can apply even when the work looks similar to a private-sector internship.
  • Pay and hours. CPT and similar programs often have rules about full-time versus part-time work and about whether the internship is required for your degree. Check those with your school before you accept.

Build a short, honest tracker

Add a column for sponsorship language and another for the date you last confirmed it. When you talk to a recruiter, record the name and what they said. If a team cannot sponsor this cycle, leave the door closed gracefully and put your energy on employers whose postings are clearer.

Around five thoughtful applications a week is a sustainable starting point during the semester. It is better to submit complete, eligible applications than to send 30 to employers whose postings already rule you out.

Talk to your school early

Before offer season, meet your international student office and ask how long CPT or the equivalent takes to approve, what documents an employer has to provide, and how that lines up with typical start dates. Bring a sample offer letter if you have one. Hiring teams move faster when you can tell them the exact steps and a realistic timeline.

The goal is a short list of internships you are actually eligible for, with the eligibility confirmed on the employer's page. That list will be smaller than an unfiltered search. It is also the one worth your time.
