Engineering internships

How to get an engineering internship

Where engineering interns get hired, how to build hands-on proof, the interview, and how to find roles at local manufacturers and firms.

By Amney Mounir. Published November 16, 2026.

Engineering internships in mechanical, electrical, civil, chemical, and industrial engineering work a little differently from software roles. Fewer of them are posted on the big job boards, more of them sit at manufacturers, utilities, and construction firms you may never have heard of, and hands-on projects count for a lot. Here is how I would go after one as a student today.

Know where engineering interns get hired

Most engineering internships are not at famous brands. They are at:

  • Manufacturers: auto parts, aerospace suppliers, food production, medical devices, and packaging plants.
  • Utilities and energy companies: power, water, oil and gas, and renewables.
  • Construction and civil firms: general contractors, design consultants, and transportation agencies.
  • Government and public agencies: state departments of transportation, city engineering offices, and national labs.
  • Product and hardware companies: electronics, robotics, and consumer goods.

Many of these companies only post openings on their own career pages, so searching beyond the big boards matters more in engineering than almost anywhere else.

Build hands-on proof

Recruiters want to see that you can build, test, and fix things. A few ways to show it:

  • Join a project team like Formula SAE, Baja, robotics, concrete canoe, solar car, or Engineers Without Borders, and take ownership of one part.
  • Do a personal project with real constraints, like a 3D-printed part, an Arduino sensor build, or a small home repair you designed and documented.
  • Learn the tools for your field: CAD (SolidWorks, Fusion 360, or AutoCAD), MATLAB, and basic Excel. Electrical students should be comfortable with a multimeter and a breadboard.

Take photos and keep short notes as you go. They make great interview material later.

Write a resume that shows what you built

Keep it to one page. Put projects near the top if you do not have work experience yet. For each one, say what you did and what happened:

  • "Designed and machined a brake pedal bracket that cut weight by 20%"
  • "Built a temperature logger with an Arduino and three sensors, used by our lab for a semester"

Add a short skills line with software, lab equipment, and any certifications like OSHA 10 or the FE exam if you have them.

Prepare for the interview

Engineering interviews usually mix behavioral questions with a few technical ones:

  1. "Walk me through a project you are proud of." Explain the problem, your part, and what you would change.
  2. "Tell me about a time something did not work." Talk about how you found the cause.
  3. Basic technical questions from your coursework, like statics, circuits, or thermodynamics.
  4. "Why this company?" Know what they make and who their customers are.

Bring a printed copy of your resume and, if it helps, a few photos of your projects on your phone.

Timing

Large engineering employers often recruit in the fall, especially at career fairs. Smaller manufacturers, contractors, and local agencies keep hiring through the spring, often until April or May. If fall recruiting did not work out, keep applying.

Search more job titles

Engineering internships go by many names. Try mechanical engineering intern, electrical engineering intern, manufacturing engineering intern, process engineering intern, quality engineering intern, field engineering intern, and co-op.

To find them, I Got An Internship is a free internship search tool that pulls openings straight from about 2,200 employer career pages across the US and Canada. Search for engineering internships in your city and you will find roles at local manufacturers and firms that never make it to the big job boards.

A simple plan

  • Pick one or two engineering fields and the types of employers you want
  • Join a project team or build one personal project
  • Learn the main software for your field
  • Write a one-page resume built around what you made
  • Apply to 40 or more roles, including smaller local companies and co-ops
