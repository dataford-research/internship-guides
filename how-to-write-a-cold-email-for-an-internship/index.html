How to write a cold email for an internship

Who to email, how long it should be, a template you can copy, and how many to send before you hear back.

By Amney Mounir. Published November 16, 2026.

Most students only apply through the online form. That works sometimes, but a short, specific email to a real person can get your application read by someone who can actually move it forward. It is also how a lot of internships at small companies and startups get filled, because many of them never post the role at all. Here is how I would write one.

Pick the right person

The best person to email is usually not the CEO or a general HR inbox. Aim for someone close to the work:

  • A manager or team lead on the team you want to join
  • A recent intern or junior employee on that team
  • A recruiter who lists university or early-career hiring in their LinkedIn headline
  • At a small company, a founder is fine, since they often do the hiring themselves

You can find most of these people on LinkedIn by searching the company name plus the team or role. Work emails often follow a pattern like first.last@company.com, and the company's press page or blog sometimes shows it.

Keep it short

A cold email should take less than 30 seconds to read. Four to six sentences is plenty. The person you are writing to gets a lot of email, and a long message is easy to save for later and then forget.

A simple structure:

  1. Who you are, in one line
  2. Why you are writing to them specifically
  3. One or two things that show you can help
  4. A small, clear ask
  5. A thank you

Write a subject line that says what it is

Plain works best. For example:

  • "Rising junior interested in data internships at [Company]"
  • "Question about summer 2027 marketing internships"
  • "[Your school] student, applied to the software engineering intern role"

Avoid vague subject lines like "Quick question" or "Opportunity". They look like spam.

A template you can copy

"Hi [Name],

I am a junior studying [major] at [school], and I am looking for a summer 2027 internship in [field]. I read about [specific project, product, or post] and it is the kind of work I want to learn.

This past year I [one concrete thing, like built a project, ran a club event, or worked a part-time job] and [one more relevant detail]. I put my resume below.

Are you open to a 15-minute call, or could you point me to the right person to talk to about internships on your team?

Thanks for your time,

[Your name]

[LinkedIn or portfolio link]"

Swap in details that are true for you. The specific line about their work is the part that makes the email feel written for them and not sent to 200 people.

Make the ask small

Asking "Can I have an internship?" puts a lot on a stranger. Asking for 15 minutes, a pointer to the right person, or whether they would take a look at your application is much easier to say yes to. If the call goes well, the internship conversation usually comes up on its own.

Follow up once

If you do not hear back in about a week, send one short follow up in the same thread:

"Hi [Name], just bumping this in case it got buried. I would still love to learn more about internships on your team. Thanks again."

After that, move on. No reply is normal and usually just means they are busy.

Send enough of them

Cold email is a numbers game. If you send five, you might hear nothing. If you send 30 to 50 over a few weeks, you will usually get a few replies and at least one real conversation. Keep a simple list of who you emailed, the date, and whether they answered, so you know when to follow up.

To build that list faster, I Got An Internship is a free internship search tool that pulls openings from about 2,200 employer career pages across the US and Canada. Find companies that are hiring interns in your field, apply, and then email someone on the team to put a name to your application.

Quick checklist

  • Email someone close to the team, not a general inbox
  • Keep it to four to six sentences
  • Use a plain subject line that says what the email is about
  • Mention one specific thing about their work
  • Make the ask small, like a 15-minute call
  • Follow up once after about a week
  • Send 30 to 50 over a few weeks and track them
