How to get an internship as a freshman

Which programs hire first-year students, how to build one project you can point to, and how to apply wide enough.

By Amney Mounir. Published November 16, 2026.

A lot of students think internships are only for juniors and seniors. Many big programs do focus on upperclassmen, but there are more freshman options than most people expect, and the students who start early have a much easier time later. Here is how I would approach it in your first year.

Know which programs hire freshmen

Some companies run programs built for first and second year students. They usually have names like early insight, discovery, explore, or sophomore programs, and they open early, often in the fall for the next summer. Search for those terms plus your field and write down every deadline you find.

Outside of formal programs, the best freshman options tend to be:

  • Small companies and startups, where one person can decide to bring you on
  • Local businesses, nonprofits, and city or state offices
  • Research labs at your own school
  • Campus jobs that use real skills, like IT help desk, the student newspaper, or the marketing office

Build one thing you can point to

As a freshman you will not have much work experience, and that is fine. What helps most is one concrete thing you made or did. A few ideas:

  • A class project you can explain in two sentences, with a link if possible
  • A small side project, like a website, a spreadsheet model, or a short research write-up
  • A club role where you owned something, even if it was small

Put it at the top of your resume under a Projects section. A recruiter would rather see one real project than a long list of coursework.

Keep your resume to one page

Use your school, expected graduation date, relevant classes, your project, any jobs (including retail or food service), and a short skills line. Keep each bullet to one line when you can and start it with a verb. Leave out high school unless it shows something you did not do in college yet.

Apply wider than you think you need to

Freshmen get fewer responses, so plan for it. I would aim for 30 to 50 applications in a season, track every one in a simple spreadsheet, and apply in the first week a role is posted. Many postings close early once they get enough applicants.

Use your school

Your career center can review your resume, run mock interviews, and tell you which employers like to hire students from your school. Go to career fairs even if you think no one will hire a freshman. Talking to recruiters is practice, and some of them will remember you next year.

Professors are another good path. Go to office hours, ask about their research, and ask if they ever take students on for a semester or summer. Lab and research roles often go to students who simply asked.

Reach out to people directly

A short, specific message works better than a long one. Something like: "I'm a freshman studying economics at my school and I'm interested in how your team does pricing research. Would you be open to a 15 minute call sometime this month?" Ask for advice, not a job. If the call goes well, ask if they know of any openings for students.

If summer doesn't work out

Plenty of freshmen do not land a summer internship, and it does not hurt you. Take a summer job, keep building your project, take a class, or volunteer somewhere related to your field. All of that gives you something to talk about when you apply again in the fall.

Quick checklist

  • Search for early insight, discovery, and sophomore programs and note the deadlines
  • Look at small companies, nonprofits, local offices, and campus research
  • Add one real project to your resume
  • Apply to 30 to 50 roles and track them
  • Visit your career center and go to office hours
  • Send short, specific messages to people in your field

Always confirm the pay, hours, and location details in writing before you accept an offer.
