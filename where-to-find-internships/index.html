Where to find internships as a college student

Employer career pages, your school platform, career fairs, professors, niche boards, and internship search tools, plus a simple weekly routine.

By Amney Mounir. Published November 16, 2026.

Most students start and end their internship search on one or two big job boards. That works for some roles, but it means you are competing with everyone else who saw the same post on the same day. The best results I have seen come from mixing a few sources and checking them on a schedule.

Here is where I would look, and how much time I would spend on each.

1. Employer career pages

This is the source of truth. Every internship that shows up on a job board started as a posting on the company's own careers site, and many never get syndicated at all, especially at mid-size companies, hospitals, utilities, and government agencies.

The catch is that checking hundreds of career pages by hand is slow. Pick your top 20 to 30 employers, bookmark their student or early-career page, and check them once a week. Set job alerts wherever the site offers them.

2. Your school's career platform

Employers who post on your school's platform (Handshake, 12twenty, Symplicity, or a custom portal) are telling you they want students from your school. Competition is usually smaller than on public boards. Check it weekly, and read the career center's event calendar too. Info sessions and employer office hours are where recruiters put faces to names.

3. Career fairs and info sessions

Go even if you do not love the employer list. Talk to two or three recruiters, ask what the team works on and when they review applications, and write down their names. That gives you a real person to follow up with later, which beats applying cold.

4. Professors, alumni, and your department

Faculty often hear about research assistant roles, lab positions, and small-company internships that are never posted publicly. Ask your professors directly. Your department newsletter and alumni groups on LinkedIn are also worth a scan every couple of weeks.

5. Niche and industry boards

If you know your field, use the boards built for it. A few examples by area:

  • Government: USAJOBS Pathways and your state's job site.
  • Nonprofits: Idealist.
  • Startups: Wellfound and startup-specific newsletters.
  • Research and science: NSF REU listings and university lab pages.

These sites have fewer listings, but a higher share of them fit.

6. Internship search tools

General job boards mix internships in with full-time roles, which makes filtering painful. A tool built only for internships saves time.

I built I Got An Internship for exactly this. It is a free internship search platform that pulls internships and co-ops straight from about 2,200 employer career pages across the US and Canada, about 5,000 roles at a time. You can filter by role, term, city, and visa sponsorship, and listings come down when the employer removes the posting, so you are not applying to roles that already closed.

How I would split the time

For a student applying during a busy season, a simple weekly routine looks like this:

  • Monday: search tools and job boards, add new roles to your tracker.
  • Wednesday: check your top employer career pages and your school platform.
  • Friday: send follow-ups, reach out to one alum or recruiter, and review your tracker.

Two or three focused hours a week, done every week, beats one panicked weekend.

Track everything in one place

Whatever sources you use, put every application in one spreadsheet: company, role, link, date applied, status, and follow-up date. When you have 40 applications going, you will not remember which ones you already followed up on. A tracker also shows you which sources actually get you interviews, so you can spend more time there next season.

Always confirm dates, eligibility, and pay on the employer's own posting before you apply.
