How to write an internship cover letter

A short four-part structure, examples you can adapt, and a checklist before you submit.

By Amney Mounir. Published November 16, 2026.

A lot of internship applications list the cover letter as optional, and a lot of students skip it. When it is optional, a short and specific letter can still help, especially at smaller companies where a person reads every application. When it is required, a generic letter is easy to spot. Here is the simple structure I recommend.

Keep it short

Aim for three or four short paragraphs, around 250 to 350 words. It should fit on one page with room to spare. Recruiters reading intern applications are often moving through many of them in a sitting, so get to the point quickly.

Paragraph 1: the role and why you are writing

Name the exact internship title and term, and say in one or two sentences why this company. Use something real: a product you use, a team you read about, a project on their engineering or research blog. Avoid opening lines like "I am writing to express my interest."

Example: "I am applying for the Summer 2027 Data Analyst Intern role. I have used your budgeting app for two years, and your recent post on how the team measures retention is the reason I want to work on that problem."

Paragraph 2: one or two pieces of proof

Pick the one or two experiences that best match the posting and tell them briefly. A class project, a part-time job, a club role, or a personal project all work. Say what you did, the tools you used, and the result.

Example: "In my statistics course I led a four-person project analyzing 3 years of campus dining data in Python. We found two menu changes that cut food waste, and the dining office used our report in its spring planning."

Read the posting first and mirror the two or three skills it asks for most, in its own words where they honestly describe what you did.

Paragraph 3: what you want to learn and add

Interns are expected to learn. Say what you hope to learn in the role and what you can contribute from day one, such as reliability, quick learning, a specific tool, or experience working with customers.

Closing: thank them and make it easy

Thank them, say you would welcome the chance to talk, and include your email. That is enough. No need to repeat your whole resume.

A few rules that help

  • Address a real person if you can. Check the posting, the company team page, or LinkedIn. If you cannot find a name, "Dear Hiring Team" is fine.
  • Write a new first paragraph for each company. You can reuse your proof paragraph across similar roles, but the opening should be specific.
  • Match the tone of the company. A startup and a bank read differently. Keep it professional either way.
  • Save it as a PDF with your name in the file name, unless the form asks for a text box.
  • Read it out loud once before you submit. It is the fastest way to catch typos.

Spend most of your time finding the right roles

A good cover letter helps, but only if it goes to roles that are still open and that fit you. Most students need somewhere around 30 to 80 applications in a season, so it helps to find postings quickly.

It is a free internship search platform that pulls internships and co-ops straight from about 2,200 employer career pages across the US and Canada, about 5,000 roles at a time. You can filter by role, term, city, and visa sponsorship, and listings are removed when the employer takes the posting down.

Quick checklist

  • Exact role title and term in the first line
  • One real reason you want this company
  • One or two pieces of proof with results
  • What you want to learn and what you bring
  • Under one page, saved as a PDF
  • No typos

Always confirm the requirements, dates, and pay on the employer's own posting before you apply.
