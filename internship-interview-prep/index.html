How to prepare for an internship interview

The common interview formats, how to research the company, the stories to prepare, and a checklist for the day of.

By Amney Mounir. Published November 16, 2026.

Getting the interview is the hard part. Most internship interviews follow a predictable pattern, so a few hours of focused prep goes a long way. Here is the plan I give students, whether it is a 20 minute recruiter screen or a final round with the team.

Know what kind of interview it is

Ask the recruiter, or check the invite, so you prepare for the right thing. Most internship processes use some mix of these:

  • Recruiter or phone screen. 15 to 30 minutes. Your background, why this company, availability, and work authorization.
  • Behavioral interview. Questions like "tell me about a time you worked on a team" or "tell me about a mistake you made."
  • Technical or case interview. Coding, SQL, a finance or consulting case, a design exercise, or questions on your coursework, depending on the role.
  • Team or hiring manager interview. A mix of the above, plus a lot of "what do you want to learn here."

If you are not sure, it is fine to reply to the invite and ask what format to expect. Recruiters get that question all the time.

Research the company for 30 minutes

You do not need to memorize the annual report. You need enough to answer "why us" with something real:

  • What the company sells and who its customers are
  • What the team you would join works on
  • One recent piece of news, a product launch, or a blog post you found interesting

Write down two or three sentences you could say out loud. That is your "why this company" answer.

Prepare 5 or 6 stories

Most behavioral questions can be answered with the same handful of stories. Pick 5 or 6 experiences from classes, projects, part-time jobs, clubs, or volunteering, and make sure you have at least one for each of these:

  • Working on a team
  • A problem you solved
  • A mistake or failure and what you learned
  • Leading something or taking initiative
  • Handling a tight deadline

Use a simple structure for each one: the situation, what you did, and the result. Keep each story under two minutes. Say what you did, not just what the group did, and include a number if you have one.

Practice the questions that come up the most

These come up in almost every internship interview:

  • Tell me about yourself
  • Why do you want this internship?
  • Why this company?
  • Tell me about a project you are proud of
  • What is a weakness you are working on?
  • Do you have any questions for us?

For "tell me about yourself," aim for about 60 to 90 seconds: what you study, one or two things you have worked on, and why this role is the next step.

Practice out loud, not just in your head. Recording yourself once on your phone is uncomfortable but it is the fastest way to notice filler words and answers that run long.

Prep the technical part for your field

If the role has a technical round, spend most of your prep time here:

  • Software engineering: easy and medium coding problems, plus talking through your approach as you go.
  • Data and analytics: SQL joins and aggregations, basic statistics, and walking through a project you analyzed.
  • Finance: accounting basics, how the three statements connect, and a market or company you follow.
  • Consulting: practice cases with a friend, and simple math done out loud.

Interviewers at the internship level mostly want to see how you think. If you get stuck, say what you are considering instead of going quiet.

Have 2 or 3 questions ready

Asking good questions at the end shows you are serious. A few that work well:

  • What does a typical week look like for an intern on this team?
  • What did past interns work on?
  • What would make someone really successful in this role?

The day before and the day of

  • Test your camera, microphone, and the video link if it is virtual
  • Have a copy of your resume and the job posting open
  • Join or arrive 5 to 10 minutes early
  • Keep a glass of water and a notepad nearby

Send a short thank-you note

Within 24 hours, send a brief email to each interviewer, or to the recruiter if you do not have their emails. Thank them, mention one thing you talked about, and restate that you are excited about the role. Three or four sentences is plenty.

Keep applying while you wait

One interview is not an offer, so keep your pipeline full. Most students need somewhere around 30 to 80 applications in a season to land a few interviews.

Quick checklist

  • Know the interview format
  • 30 minutes of company research and a "why us" answer
  • 5 or 6 stories ready
  • Practiced out loud at least once
  • Technical prep for your field
  • 2 or 3 questions for them
  • Thank-you note within 24 hours

Always confirm the requirements, dates, and pay on the employer's own posting before you accept an offer.
