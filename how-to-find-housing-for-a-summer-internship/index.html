Internship housing

How to find housing for a summer internship

What to ask your company, where interns actually find short-term places, how to split costs, and how to avoid housing scams.

By Amney Mounir. Published November 16, 2026.

Getting the offer is the hard part, but a lot of students hit a second wall right after: the internship is in another city and you have about 10 to 12 weeks to fill with a place to live. Short-term housing is awkward because most leases are 12 months, and summer is when every intern in town is looking at the same time. Here is how I would handle it.

Ask the company first

Before you search anywhere, ask your recruiter what the company offers. Many larger internship programs give a housing stipend, a relocation bonus, or corporate housing that they book for you. Some also have a list of places past interns stayed or a private group where this year's interns find roommates.

You can ask it simply:

"Does the internship program include housing, a housing stipend, or relocation support? And is there a way to connect with other interns who will be in the same city?"

If the answer is a stipend, find out whether it is paid up front or with your first paycheck. That changes how much cash you need for a deposit.

Know your budget before you look

Take your expected take-home pay for the summer, subtract any stipend gaps, and decide on a rent number you are comfortable with. In expensive cities like New York, San Francisco, Seattle, or Boston, interns often end up sharing a room or living farther out with a good transit line. Being clear on your budget early saves you from falling for listings you cannot afford.

Look at summer housing at local universities

This is the option most students forget. Many universities rent out dorm rooms over the summer to interns, even if you do not go to that school. It is usually furnished, close to transit, and priced by the week or month. Search for "summer intern housing" plus the city or university name, and apply early, because these fill up by spring.

Use sublets from students who are leaving

Summer is when local students leave town, and many of them want someone to take over their lease for June through August. Good places to look are university sublet groups, local subreddits, and roommate apps. Sublets are often cheaper than anything else, and the place is already furnished.

Ask to video call the person, see the room live, and confirm the landlord allows sublets. Get the agreement in writing.

Try furnished monthly rentals

Furnished apartments that rent by the month are more expensive, but they are simple: one booking, utilities included, and no furniture to buy. They make the most sense if your company gives you a stipend or if you are splitting the place with another intern.

Find roommates who are also interning

Splitting a two or three bedroom place with other interns is often the best mix of price and comfort. Ask your recruiter if there is an intern group chat, post in your school's alumni network for that city, and check LinkedIn for people who posted that they will be interning at the same company.

Avoid the common scams

Short-term housing attracts scams, and students are an easy target. A few rules:

  • Never wire money or pay with gift cards
  • Do not pay a deposit for a place you have not seen live, even on video
  • Be careful with listings that are far below the going rate for the area
  • Search the address and the photos online to see if the listing is copied
  • Get a written agreement with the dates, rent, and deposit terms

Start early

The best summer housing is gone by April or May in most big cities. Once you sign your offer, give yourself a week to ask the company about support, then start looking right away. If you are still deciding between offers, factor housing cost into that decision too. A higher hourly rate can disappear fast in a city where rent is double.

If you are still in the search stage, I Got An Internship is a free internship search tool that pulls openings from about 2,200 employer career pages across the US and Canada. You can filter by city, so you can see which roles are close to where you already live or where housing is easier to find.

Quick checklist

  • Ask your recruiter about housing, stipends, and an intern group chat
  • Set a rent budget based on your real take-home pay
  • Check summer dorm rentals at local universities
  • Look for student sublets from June to August
  • Consider furnished monthly rentals if you have a stipend
  • Split a place with other interns when you can
  • Never wire money or pay for a place you have not seen
  • Start looking as soon as you sign the offer
