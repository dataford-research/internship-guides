How to choose between two internship offers

What to compare, questions to ask each team, return offer odds, the money math, and how to ask for more time.

By Amney Mounir. Published November 16, 2026.

Getting two internship offers at the same time is a good problem, but it can still be stressful. One deadline is usually closer than the other, and it is hard to compare a big-name company with a smaller team that seems more hands-on. Here is the simple process I walk students through when they are deciding.

Write down what you want from the summer

Before you compare offers, spend ten minutes on what you want out of the internship. Most students care about some mix of these:

  • The kind of work you will do day to day
  • Whether it can turn into a full-time return offer
  • The name on your resume
  • Pay, housing, and cost of living in the city
  • Who your manager and team will be
  • Skills you want to build for the next job

Rank your top three. That list does most of the deciding for you.

Compare the actual work

The job title does not tell you much. Two "data analyst intern" roles can be completely different. Ask each recruiter or manager:

  • What projects did last summer's interns work on?
  • Will I own a project from start to finish?
  • Who will I report to, and how often will we meet?
  • What tools and skills will I use most?

An internship where you ship one real project you can talk about in interviews is usually worth more than one where you sit in meetings all summer.

Look at return offer odds

If you are a junior, the return offer matters a lot. Ask how many interns got full-time offers last year. Many large companies convert a big share of their interns, and that can take a lot of pressure off your senior year. If you are a first or second year, the experience itself matters more than the return offer.

Do the money math

Compare take-home pay after rent, travel, and food, not just the hourly rate. A higher rate in an expensive city can leave you with less than a lower rate in a cheaper one. Check whether either company offers a housing stipend or relocation help.

Talk to someone who worked there

Find one or two former interns on LinkedIn and send a short message asking for 15 minutes. Ask what a normal week looked like and whether they would do it again. This is the best way to learn about the manager and the team, which the offer letter will never tell you.

Handle the deadlines

If your preferred company has not decided yet and the other offer has a deadline, it is fine to ask for more time. Be honest and polite:

> Thank you again for the offer. I am very excited about it. I am waiting to hear back from one other company and wanted to ask if it would be possible to extend my deadline by a week so I can make a final decision.

Many employers will give you a few extra days. If they cannot, you have to decide with the information you have. Once you accept, do not back out for a different offer. Reneging can hurt your reputation and your school's relationship with the employer.

Keep looking until you sign

If you only have one offer and you are not sure about it, keep applying until you have something you are happy with. A lot of internships are posted on employer career pages and get far less traffic than the big job boards.

A quick checklist before you decide

  • Rank your top three priorities for the summer
  • Ask each team about real projects and who you report to
  • Ask about return offer rates
  • Compare pay after rent and travel
  • Talk to one former intern at each company
  • Ask for more time if you need it, and never renege after accepting

Always confirm the start date, pay, and requirements in writing before you accept an offer.
