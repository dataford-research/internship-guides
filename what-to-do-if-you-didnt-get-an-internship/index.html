Planning

What to do if you didn't get a summer internship

Late-season applications, part-time and fall roles, research, projects, and how to use the summer to get ready for next season.

By Amney Mounir. Published November 16, 2026.

Every spring I hear from students who applied to dozens of internships and ended up with nothing for the summer. It feels like a setback, and it is a common one. Most students do not land a big-name internship, especially in their first or second year. What matters is what you do with the next few months. Here is the plan I give students in that spot.

Keep applying through late spring

A lot of students stop applying in March because they think the season is over. Large companies do fill most spots early, but plenty of employers hire later:

  • Small and mid-size companies that only post when a team gets budget
  • Startups that hire a few weeks before the start date
  • Nonprofits, local government offices, and research labs
  • Companies that had a candidate back out and need to fill the spot fast

Keep a short daily routine going through May and even early June. Five good applications a week to roles posted in the last two weeks will do more than fifty applications to postings that have been up since October.

Ask about part-time and fall roles

If the summer slots are gone, ask whether the team takes part-time interns during the school year or a fall or spring co-op. Many employers have less competition for those terms. One semester of part-time work can carry the same weight on your resume as a summer internship.

Get research or campus experience

Your own school is one of the easiest places to get real experience:

  • Email two or three professors whose work you find interesting and ask if they need help with a project over the summer
  • Check your school's undergraduate research office for paid summer programs
  • Ask the career center, IT help desk, library, or admissions office about summer student roles

Research and campus jobs give you concrete projects to talk about in interviews next year.

Build one project you can show

If you are in a technical or analytical field, a single solid project can fill the gap. Pick something small and finish it:

  • A data analysis of a public dataset with a short write-up of what you found
  • A simple web app that solves a problem you or your friends actually have
  • A marketing plan or case study for a local business you know
  • A financial model of a public company you follow

Put it on GitHub, a personal site, or a one-page PDF. When an interviewer asks what you did last summer, you will have a specific answer.

Take any job and learn from it

A summer job at a restaurant, retail store, camp, or warehouse is real work experience. You learn how to show up, deal with customers, handle pressure, and work on a team. On your resume, focus on results: shifts covered, sales numbers, training new hires, or problems you fixed.

Use the time to prepare for next season

The best use of a summer without an internship is getting ready for the next round. Applications for many summer internships open in late summer and early fall, so you have a few months to:

  • Rewrite your resume with whatever you did this summer
  • Practice common interview questions out loud
  • Reach out to alumni at the companies you want
  • Build a list of target companies and note when each one posted last year

Set up a better search for next time

When students tell me they could not find anything, the problem is often that they were checking the same few job boards as everyone else. A lot of internships are posted on employer career pages and never get much traffic.

I built I Got An Internship for this. It is a free internship search platform that pulls internships and co-ops straight from about 2,200 employer career pages across the US and Canada, about 5,000 roles at a time. You can filter by role, term, city, and visa sponsorship, and listings come down when the employer removes the posting. It is a quick way to see what is still open late in the season and to spot new postings as soon as they go up.

A simple summer plan

  • Weeks 1 to 2: send late-season applications and ask about part-time or fall roles
  • Weeks 3 to 4: line up a research project, campus role, or summer job
  • Weeks 5 to 10: work, build one project, and keep notes on what you did
  • Weeks 11 to 12: update your resume and start your list for next season

Missing one summer is not the end of anything. Students who use it well often go into the next season with a stronger resume than the ones who had a short internship and did nothing with it.

Always confirm the requirements, dates, and pay on the employer's own posting before you accept an offer.
