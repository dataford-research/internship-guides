Networking

How to network for an internship as a college student

Who to contact first, a short message you can copy, what to ask on the call, and how to ask for a referral without making it awkward.

By Amney Mounir. Published November 16, 2026.

A lot of internship offers start with a conversation, not an application. That does not mean you need a famous parent or a big LinkedIn following. It means you need a short list of people, a simple message, and a habit of following up. Here is the approach I give students who feel like they have no network at all.

Start with the people you already have

Your network is bigger than you think. Before you message strangers, write down everyone in these groups:

  • Professors and teaching assistants from classes you did well in
  • Your school's career center advisors
  • Alumni from your school, your major, or your high school
  • Upperclassmen who interned somewhere last summer
  • Managers and coworkers from part-time jobs
  • Club advisors, coaches, and people you volunteered with
  • Family friends and friends' parents

You are not asking any of them for a job. You are asking for advice, a 15 minute call, or the name of someone they think you should talk to. That is a much easier yes.

Use your school's alumni as your main list

Alumni are the best cold contacts for students because you already have something in common. A few places to find them:

  • LinkedIn: search the company name, then filter people by your school
  • Your school's alumni directory or mentoring platform, if it has one
  • Club and fraternity or sorority alumni groups
  • Speakers at career center events and employer info sessions

Aim for people one to five years out of school. They remember what the internship search felt like, and they are close enough to the hiring process to give useful advice.

Send a short, specific message

Most networking messages fail because they are long or vague. Keep it to four or five sentences:

> Hi Jordan, I'm a sophomore studying economics at [school] and saw you interned at [company] before joining full time. I'm applying for summer 2027 analyst internships and would really value 15 minutes to hear how you prepared and what the team looks for. Would you be open to a quick call in the next couple of weeks? Totally understand if you're busy.

What makes this work:

  • It says who you are and how you found them
  • It names the role and the season you are targeting
  • It asks for something small and specific
  • It gives them an easy way to say no

Do not attach your resume to the first message, and do not ask for a referral before you have talked.

Make the call worth their time

Prepare four or five questions before the call:

  • How did you get your internship there?
  • What does a typical week look like for an intern on your team?
  • What skills made the biggest difference in your first few months?
  • Is there anything you wish you had known when you applied?
  • Is there anyone else you think I should talk to?

That last question is the most important one. One good conversation often turns into two more. Keep the call to the time you asked for, and take notes.

Follow up and ask for a referral the right way

Send a thank-you message the same day. Mention one specific thing you learned. If you are applying to their company, a referral request can come after the call:

> Thanks again for the time today. Your advice about practicing SQL questions was really helpful. I'm planning to apply to the summer analyst internship this week. If you are comfortable, would you be open to referring me? I'm happy to send my resume and the job link.

If they say no or do not reply, that is fine. Thank them and move on. Keep them updated every few months when something changes, like a new project or an offer.

Keep track of who you talked to

Networking falls apart without a list. Add a few columns to the same spreadsheet you use for applications: name, company, how you know them, date contacted, date of call, next follow-up, and notes. Once a week, check who needs a follow-up.

Pair networking with a steady application routine

Networking helps most when you are already applying to a steady list of roles. When someone mentions an opening, you want to be ready to apply that day.

I built I Got An Internship to make the search side faster. It is a free internship search platform that pulls internships and co-ops straight from about 2,200 employer career pages across the US and Canada, about 5,000 roles at a time. You can filter by role, term, city, and visa sponsorship, and listings come down when the employer removes the posting. When an alum tells you their team is hiring interns, you can check what is open and apply right away.

A simple weekly plan

  • Monday: add 5 new people to your list
  • Tuesday: send 3 to 5 short messages
  • Wednesday to Friday: take calls and send thank-you notes
  • Friday: update your tracker and send any follow-ups

Do this for a month and you will have more conversations than most students have in a full year.

Always confirm the requirements, dates, and pay on the employer's own posting before you accept an offer.
